# Stoic primer: how tiny-stoic judges, after Epictetus

The Stoic labels in tiny-stoic follow Epictetus. Where the Stoics disagree, we take his side. He judges a person by the judgement they act or feel from, over what is up to them, in their role; he speaks to people making progress, not to sages. Part 1 is his doctrine, in his words (fragment 9 survives only in Aulus Gellius's summary), with the earlier Stoics where he is silent, marked; the Stoic-instructed eval reads exactly Part 1. Part 2 is how we turn it into dataset labels, with the scholarship behind each rule.

Translations in Part 1 are public domain: Epictetus tr. W. A. Oldfather (Loeb, 1925-28); Diogenes Laertius tr. R. D. Hicks (1925). Arius Didymus: our copy names no translator; it is attributed to A. J. Pomeroy (1999), unchecked. Every quote is checked against its source text by `scripts/13_quote_check.py`.

## What is up to us

> 1. Some things are under our control, while others are not under our control. Under our control are conception, choice, desire, aversion, and, in a word, everything that is our own doing; not under our control are our body, our property, reputation, office, and, in a word, everything that is not our own doing.
>
> (Epictetus, *Handbook* 1)

> As was fitting, therefore, the gods have put under our control only the most excellent faculty of all and that which dominates the rest, namely, the power to make correct use of external impressions, but all the others they have not put under our control.
>
> (*Discourses* 1.1)

Good and bad are found only there:

> The essence of the good is a certain kind of moral purpose, and that of the evil is a certain kind of moral purpose. What, then, are the external things? They are materials for the moral purpose, in dealing with which it will find its own proper good or evil.
>
> (*Discourses* 1.29)

## Acting well and acting badly

> 5. It is not the things themselves that disturb men, but their judgements about these things. For example, death is nothing dreadful, or else Socrates too would have thought so, but the judgement that death is dreadful, *this* is the dreadful thing.
>
> (*Handbook* 5)

> He is hasty about bathing. — Is all well, then? — That by no means follows; but only the act which proceeds from correct judgements is well done, and that which proceeds from bad judgements is badly done. Yet until you learn the judgement from which a man performs each separate act, neither praise his action nor blame it.
>
> (*Discourses* 4.8)

> 45. Somebody is hasty about bathing; do not say that he bathes badly, but that he is hasty about bathing. Somebody drinks a good deal of wine; do not say that he drinks badly, but that he drinks a good deal. For until you have decided what judgement prompts him, how do you know that what he is doing is bad?
>
> (*Handbook* 45)

A sincere judgement can still be wrong:

> 42. When someone treats you ill or speaks ill of you, remember that he acts or speaks thus because he thinks it is incumbent upon him. That being the case, it is impossible for him to follow what appears good to you, but what appears good to himself; whence it follows, that, if he gets a wrong view of things, the man that suffers is the man that has been deceived.
>
> (*Handbook* 42)

> Wherefore, we lie, indeed, but are ready with the arguments which prove that one ought not to lie.
>
> (*Handbook* 52)

> And in the same way we shall declare the same thing to be the cause of our good actions.
>
> (*Discourses* 1.11, after: "whenever we do anything wrongly ... we shall ascribe to this action no other cause than the decision of our will which led us to do it")

## The three fields of training

> There are three fields of study in which the man who is going to be good and excellent must first have been trained. The first has to do with desires and aversions, that he may never fail to get what he desires, nor fall into what he avoids; the second with cases of choice and of refusal, and, in general, with duty, that he may act in an orderly fashion, upon good reasons, and not carelessly; the third with the avoidance of error and rashness in judgement, and, in general, about cases of assent. Among these the most important and especially pressing is that which has to do with the stronger emotions; for a strong emotion does not arise except a desire fails to attain its object, or an aversion falls into what it would avoid.
>
> (*Discourses* 3.2)

## The passions: fear, grief, envy, anger

They are judgements that something outside our control is good or evil.

> When I see a man in anxiety, I say to myself, What can it be that this fellow wants? For if he did not want something that was outside of his control, how could he still remain in anxiety? That is why the citharoede when singing all alone shows no anxiety, but does so when he enters the theatre, even though he has a very beautiful voice and plays the cithara admirably; for he does not wish merely to sing well, but also to win applause, and that is no longer under his control.
>
> (*Discourses* 2.13)

> For if the true nature of the good is one of the things that are under our control, there is no place for either envy or jealousy; and you yourself will not wish to be a praetor, or a senator, or a consul, but a free man.
>
> (*Handbook* 19)

> 20. Bear in mind that it is not the man who reviles or strikes you that insults you, but it is your judgement that these men are insulting you.
>
> (*Handbook* 20)

> For if the loss of the greatest things is the greatest harm that can befall a man, while the greatest thing in each man is a right moral purpose, and if a man is deprived of this very thing, what ground is left for you to be angry at him?
>
> (*Discourses* 1.18)

Affection itself is not the fault:

> — Whatever, therefore, we find to be at the same time both affectionate and reasonable, this we confidently assert to be both right and good?
>
> (*Discourses* 1.11)

Sympathy in words is right; the fault is to judge the loss an evil:

> 16. When you see someone weeping in sorrow, either because a child has gone on a journey, or because he has lost his property, beware that you be not carried away by the impression that the man is in the midst of external ills, but straightway keep before you this thought: "It is not what has happened that distresses this man (for it does not distress another), but his judgement about it." Do not, however, hesitate to sympathize with him so far as words go, and, if occasion offers, even to groan with him; but be careful not to groan also in the centre of your being.
>
> (*Handbook* 16)

## First movements are not faults

> Therefore, when some terrifying sound comes from the sky, or from the collapse of a building, or sudden word comes of some peril or other, or something else of the same sort happens, the mind of even the wise man cannot help but be disturbed, and shrink, and grow pale for a moment, not from any anticipation of some evil, but because of certain swift and unconsidered motions which forestall the action of the intellect and the reason.
>
> (Epictetus, fragment 9, as summarized by Aulus Gellius 19.1)

## What one's role asks

> 30. Our duties are in general measured by our social relationships. He is a father. One is called upon to take care of him, to give way to him in all things, to submit when he reviles or strikes you.
>
> (*Handbook* 30)

> Next, if you sit in the town council of some city, remember that you are a councillor; if you are young, remember that you are young; if old, that you are an elder; if a father, that you are a father. For each of these designations, when duly considered, always suggests the acts that are appropriate to it.
>
> (*Discourses* 2.10)

Externals are not good, but some are by nature to be preferred, and we should act for them:

> But as it is, seeing that we do not know beforehand what is going to happen, it is our duty to cleave to that which is naturally more fit to be chosen, since we are born for this purpose.
>
> (*Discourses* 2.10)

## Progress

> Where, then, is progress? If any man among you, withdrawing from external things, has turned his attention to the question of his own moral purpose, cultivating and perfecting it so as to make it finally harmonious with nature, elevated, free, unhindered, untrammelled, faithful, and honourable; and if he has learned that he who craves or shuns the things that are not under his control can be neither faithful nor free, but must himself of necessity be changed and tossed to and fro with them, and must end by subordinating himself to others, those, namely, who are able to procure or prevent these things that he craves or shuns; and if, finally, when he rises in the morning he proceeds to keep and observe all this that he has learned; if he bathes as a faithful man, eats as a self-respecting man, — similarly, whatever the subject matter may be with which he has to deal, putting into practice his guiding principles, as the runner does when he applies the principles of running, and the voice-trainer when he applies the principles of voice-training, — this is the man who in all truth is making progress, and the man who has not travelled at random is this one.
>
> (*Discourses* 1.4)

## The school's terms

Epictetus speaks plainly; the school's technical terms, which Oldfather often renders "duty" (*kathēkon*), are given by the earlier Stoics. Appropriate act (*kathēkon*):

> Furthermore, the term Duty is applied to that for which, when done, a reasonable defence can be adduced, e.g. harmony in the tenor of life's process, which indeed pervades the growth of plants and animals.

> Befitting acts are all those which reason prevails with us to do; and this is the case with honouring one's parents, brothers and country, and intercourse with friends. Unbefitting, or contrary to duty, are all acts that reason deprecates, e.g. to neglect one's parents, to be indifferent to one's brothers, not to agree with friends, to disregard the interests of one's country, and so forth.
>
> (Diogenes Laertius, *Lives* 7.107-108, tr. Hicks)

Right act (*katorthōma*) and error (*hamartēma*):

> Of appropriate acts, they say that some are complete — these are also spoken of as right acts. Right acts are activations in accord with virtue, such as being intelligent and acting justly. Acts which are not such are not right acts and they do not call them complete appropriate acts either, but intermediates: for example, marrying, serving as an ambassador, discussing matters, and the like.
>
> (Arius Didymus, *Epitome* 8)

> A wrong act is something done contrary to correct reason or where some appropriate act has been omitted by a rational creature.
>
> (Arius Didymus, *Epitome* 11a)

Preferred and rejected indifferents:

> Of things indifferent, as they express it, some are "preferred," others "rejected." Such as have value, they say, are "preferred," while such as have negative, instead of positive, value are "rejected."
>
> (Diogenes Laertius 7.105)

Good feelings (*eupatheiai*), which are not passions:

> Also they say that there are three emotional states which are good, namely, joy, caution, and wishing. Joy, the counterpart of pleasure, is rational elation; caution, the counterpart of fear, rational avoidance; for though the wise man will never feel fear, he will yet use caution. And they make wishing the counterpart of desire (or craving), inasmuch as it is rational appetency.
>
> (Diogenes Laertius 7.116)

## Acts that are neither

Earlier Stoics, as Diogenes Laertius reports them:

> Acts which fall under neither of the foregoing classes are those which reason neither urges us to do nor forbids, such as picking up a twig, holding a style or a scraper, and the like.
>
> (Diogenes Laertius, *Lives* 7.109, tr. Hicks)

## The four virtues

Epictetus seldom names them; the earlier Stoics, in Arius Didymus's summary:

> There are four which are primary: intelligence, self-restraint, bravery, and justice. Intelligence deals with appropriate acts; self-restraint deals with man's impulses; bravery deals with acts of endurance; justice deals with the apportioning of what is due.

> to intelligence are subordinated soundness of judgment, circumspection, shrewdness, sensibleness, <soundness of aim>, and ingenuity; to self-restraint are subordinated orderliness, propriety, modesty, and self-control; to bravery are subordinated perseverance, intrepidness, great-heartedness, stout-heartedness, and industriousness; to justice are subordinated piety, kindness, good fellowship, and fair dealing.
>
> (Arius Didymus, *Epitome of Stoic Ethics* 5b2, in Stobaeus 2.7; translation attributed to Pomeroy)

> Kindness is a knowledge which is disposed to do good. Good fellowship is a knowledge of equality in partnership. Fair dealing is a knowledge of how to deal with one's neighbors without incurring blame.
>
> (5b2)

In English: intelligence = wisdom, self-restraint = temperance, bravery = courage. Of any appropriate act one may ask which of the four virtues it comes nearest to, and of any error, which vice it shows.

### Labels

- `valence` has three values: `virtue` (acting well), `vice` (acting badly), `neutral` (neither well nor badly). This is our convention. In strict Stoic terms only a sage's act is a right act (*katorthōma*); an ordinary person's good act is at most an appropriate act (*kathēkon*) done for the right reason. We label it `virtue` because Epictetus calls an act "well done" when it "proceeds from correct judgements" and addresses people making progress, not sages (R5). This is our convention; no expert endorses it as a technical label.
- `virtue`: the one cardinal virtue (wisdom, justice, courage, temperance) the act most approximates, or fails; `virtue_other`: others that also fit (R8).
- `common_valence` (everyday label): what most people today would say of the person, from the vignette alone. It uses no Stoic doctrine and no source text.
- `stoic_specific`: the Stoic and everyday labels differ.
- `stoic_contested`: Epictetus's framing does not settle the verdict, or the source's own verdict differs from it; `contested_note` says who holds which view (R9).
- Source: the text the situation comes from (`source`, `source_quote`). The verdict is not the source's: it is Epictetus's verdict on the act as the vignette shows it.

### R1. Judge the judgement behind the act or feeling

Primary: *Discourses* 4.8 and *Handbook* 45 (Part 1). Seddon, on *Handbook* 45, gives the case where haste is not a fault:

> But it would not be right with respect to the person who bathes quickly in order to attend to some important duty; settling for a fair compromise between bathing adequately and attending to such a duty does not mean that someone is doing anything badly.
>
> (Keith Seddon, *Epictetus' Handbook and the Tablet of Cebes*, Routledge 2006, commentary on *Handbook* 45)

Long on what is up to us:

> What is up to us is how we interpret such information, and the truth and value we attribute to it.
>
> (A. A. Long, *Epictetus: A Stoic and Socratic Guide to Life*, OUP 2002, §8.2)

So a vignette must show the judgement (the motive, what the person treats as good or bad), for praise as much as for blame. If it shows only an outward act, *Handbook* 45 says we cannot call it bad, and *Discourses* 4.8 says we cannot praise it either. Our convention: such a row is rewritten to show the judgement, or flagged; it is not labelled `neutral` by default.

### R2. An assented passion is acting badly, even with no outward deed

Primary: *Handbook* 5, 19, 20; *Discourses* 2.13 (anxiety), 3.2 (the first field). Long:

> Epictetus' claim that passions are faulty judgements fits his insistence that we should always be extremely scrupulous in our use and interpretation of external impressions. He is emphatically not saying that we should not help beggars or mourners.
>
> (Long 2002, §9.3)

Konstan, on Seneca (the same school doctrine):

> As Seneca says, "anger is put to flight by teachings [praecepta], for it is a voluntary vice [vitium] of the mind, and not among those things that are a consequence of some condition of our human lot ...

(David Konstan, "Senecan Emotions", *Cambridge Companion to Seneca*, 2015, on *De ira* 2.2; Seneca, not Epictetus, but the same school doctrine)

So "a woman who has done well all year lie awake every night dreading what her manager will say at her review" is `vice`: we read nightly dread about another's verdict, when her work is good, as the judgement that the manager's approval, which is not up to her, is a good she must have (*Discourses* 2.13, the singer who wants applause). This is our reading of the vignette, not a fact it states; sleeplessness alone would not show it.

### R3. A first movement alone is neither

Primary: fragment 9. Gill:

> The passage is an important piece of evidence for 'pre-emotions' in Stoicism and part of it forms LS 65 Y. On 'impressions' and 'assent', see Introd., pp. xiii–xiv and nn. 15–16; on 'pre-emotions' (involuntary reactions that fall short of being 'emotions' in the full sense), see LS 65 X.
>
> (Christopher Gill, notes to Epictetus, tr. Hard, OUP 2014, fragment 9)

Long:

> Even a wise man will blench under a sudden shock, but blenching is not an instance of dread, as the Stoics define that passion: 'judging that something terrible is looming.'
>
> (Long 2002, §9.4)

### R4. Tears, sympathy and good feelings are not the fault

Primary: *Handbook* 16 (said of the comforter); *Discourses* 1.11 (affection with reason is "right and good"). Long:

> First, an outward acknowledgement of the distraught person's distress, putting oneself in the other's position; and, secondly, an inward refusal 'to be carried away by the impression' that the other's situation is objectively 'bad'.
>
> (Long 2002, §9.4)

*Handbook* 16 is about the comforter, not the bereaved. No rule of Epictetus on the bereaved's own tears was found in the checked sources; Seneca (same school) separates involuntary tears from grief assented to, and we use him where Epictetus is silent (as the intro says, not a reason for `stoic_contested` by itself):

> These tears are shed due to internal pressure and involuntarily.
>
> (Seneca, *Letters* 99.19, tr. Graver and Long 2015)

Good feelings are not the fault either. Long:

> In line with his general avoidance of technicalities, he does not advance an explicit theory of the emotions, and he never refers to the concept of 'good feelings' by this official name. Implicitly, however, he strongly endorses a wide range of what we today would call positive emotions or affects: family affection, gratitude, cheerfulness, joy, and enthusiasm
>
> (Long 2002, §9.3)

So rational caution, goodwill, affection and joy (Diogenes Laertius 7.116, Part 1) are not passions; acted on with reason they can be acting well (*Discourses* 1.11: "both affectionate and reasonable ... right and good").

So tears alone do not decide the label (R1). A vignette must show which case it is: tears that come at the first blow, unwilled (`neutral`, R3 and Seneca *Letters* 99.18-19), or a judgement that the death is an evil (she says it is the worst evil that could befall her: `vice`). Elapsed time alone is not a test.

### R5. "virtue" means acting well while making progress

The strict view, which we do not use for the label:

> Compare 92.27–29, together with the remark by Chrysippus that even when one is performing all the appropriate actions, one is not virtuous until one's performance is entirely secure and consistent (Stobaeus 4.39, 906W [LS 59I]; also Cicero, *On Ends* 3.20).
>
> (Margaret Graver and A. A. Long, notes to Seneca, *Letters on Ethics*, Chicago 2015, note on 75.8)

Epictetus's emphasis, which we follow:

> We hear nothing in the discourses about the 'equality' of all faults or the ignorance and misery of everyone who has failed to achieve infallible knowledge. For Epictetus, Socrates was not an approximation to wisdom but the paradigm of a genuinely wise person. He rarely even refers to the ideal sage, and, when he does, he prefers the highly traditional expression *kalos kai agathos,* 'the excellent man', to the esoteric 'wise man'
>
> (Long 2002, §1.5)

> But that infallible ideal is not his theme in the discourses.
>
> (Long 2002, §8.4)

Brittain and Brennan note that Epictetus measured his students by their assents, not by virtue and vice:

> One advantage of this description is that it does not make any reference to vice or virtue, which are not useful measures of their progress in the employment of impressions, since students will be equally vicious throughout their training.
>
> (Charles Brittain and Tad Brennan, introduction to *Simplicius: On Epictetus Handbook 1–26*, 2002)

### R6. What is appropriate depends on the role

Primary: *Handbook* 30; *Discourses* 2.10, 4.1. Long:

> The relevant relationship is entirely one-sided: us in relation to them, not them in relation to us.
>
> (Long 2002, §9.2)

So a judge who sentences a man because the law and the man's good require it, as "a curator and guardian" and not "as an enemy" (fragment 22), is acting well in his role; a judge who sentences fairly but the text gives no reason is not yet shown to act well (R1); a son who obeys a parent in a shameful act is not (*Discourses* 4.1, Socrates and Leon).

Fragment 22 (Agrippinus) and *Discourses* 4.1 (Socrates and Leon) are the sources of two extended vignettes, so Part 1 no longer quotes them: the eval model should not be shown the verdict on a scenario it is asked about. The labeller saw this example; the same holds for core w1 in R2.

### R7. `neutral` needs positive evidence

Primary: Diogenes Laertius 7.109; fragment 9. Inwood: a type of act may be "neither", but an actual act done by a person is right or wrong:

> Types of actions are described as appropriate or non-appropri- ate; but there is always a further judgement to be made on the level of tokens: each individual action is itself either correct or wrong (katorthōma, hamartēma).
>
> (Brad Inwood, *Reading Seneca*, OUP 2005, p. 103)

There is a tension here. Diogenes Laertius sorts types of act, and some types are "neither"; Inwood says an actual act, by a person who knows what they are doing, is always right or wrong. Our convention, which takes no side on that dispute: `neutral` is for a first movement shown as involuntary (R3), or an act whose type is "neither" (picking up a twig) where the text shows no judgement about good or evil at stake. Unknown judgement is not `neutral` (R1).

### R8. One main cardinal virtue

Primary: Arius 5b2 (Part 1). Hadot:

> They mutually imply one another, as do the parts of philosophy. It is enough to practice one in order to practice them all.
>
> (Pierre Hadot, *The Inner Citadel*, tr. Chase, Harvard 2001, pp. 233-234)

SEP:

> The virtue of courage, for instance, just is knowledge of what should be endured and feared, and the Stoics argue that this virtue is inseparable from all the rest: whoever has one virtue has them all (61B-H; see Schofield 1984).
>
> (Durand, Shogry and Baltzly, "Stoicism", *Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy*, §4.2, https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/stoicism/)

Seddon on Epictetus's three capacities:

> If we are inappropriately sexually attracted to someone we can apply self-control; when facing hardships we need not succumb to despair and frustration if we bring to bear our endurance; and when abused by others we should marshal our patience (see for instance Discourses 1.25.28–9 for more on resisting abuse).
>
> (Seddon 2006, commentary on *Handbook* 10)

Honesty under justice:

> The foundation of justice, moreover, is good faith; — that is, truth and fidelity to promises and agreements.
>
> (Cicero, *De Officiis* 1.23, tr. Miller 1913; Cicero following the Stoic Panaetius)

> Justice is subdivided into piety, honesty, equity, and fair dealing.
>
> (William O. Stephens, "Stoic Ethics", *Internet Encyclopedia of Philosophy*, https://iep.utm.edu/stoiceth/)

So: endurance of hardship and fear is courage; restraint of appetite, anger and vanity is temperance; honesty, kindness and fair dealing are justice; seeing what is and is not up to us, and what to do, is wisdom. Our convention: `virtue` is the one virtue most at stake (the sources give no rule for picking one); the virtues imply each other, so `virtue_other` is often non-empty.

### R9. Disputes, and praise in the source

Where Stoics disagree, the label follows Epictetus's framing (R1-R6). Where his framing does not settle it, `stoic_contested`. The standard case:

> In deciding cases of this kind Diogenes of Babylonia, a great and highly esteemed Stoic, consistently holds one view; his pupil Antipater, a most profound scholar, holds another.
>
> (Cicero, *De Officiis* 3.51, tr. Miller 1913)

Inwood reads the case as Stoics disagreeing on how to apply shared principles:

> I do not agree, though, with her contention that Diogenes and Antipater are really addressing different issues in this debate. What she misses is that Cicero's main interest in the debate is in their common ground: that despite their disagreement on the right thing to do, they nevertheless agree on the much more important principle that one should never do what one concedes to be morally wrong.
>
> (Inwood, *Reading Seneca*, 2005, ch. 4, p. 127 n. 80)

No sale-specific disclosure rule from Epictetus was found in the checked sources (source lane L5, Q41); deceit for profit is acting badly (*Handbook* 52), but silence alone is not settled. Such rows are `stoic_contested`. Our conventions here: Epictetus first, and `stoic_contested` marks three different things, named in `contested_note`: the Stoics disagree; Epictetus's framing does not settle the case; the source's own verdict differs from his.

Many sources are not Stoic (Cicero writes as an Academic, Plutarch as a Platonist critic, Diogenes Laertius as a reporter). Their praise is not by itself a Stoic verdict; ordinary praise ("brave", "just") is also not a claim of a sage's perfect virtue:

> And when the two Decii or the two Scipios are mentioned as "brave men" or Fabricius is called "the just," it is not at all that the former are quoted as perfect models of courage or the latter as a perfect model of justice, as if we had in one of them the ideal "wise man."
>
> (Cicero, *De Officiis* 3.16, tr. Miller 1913)
